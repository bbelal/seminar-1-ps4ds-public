Question

A university checks essays with an AI detector. 1% of essays are written by AI. The detector flags 90% of AI essays, and it also wrongly flags 9% of human essays. An essay is flagged. What is the chance that it was written by AI?

A≈ 90%
B≈ 50%
C≈ 9%
D≈ 1%

Count 1,000 essays

Flagged: 9 + 89 = 98. AI among them: 9.  →  9 / 98 ≈ 9.2%  ·  Answer C

Notation for today

We follow the book Probability and Statistics for Data Science (C. Fernandez-Granda), section 4.8.

A tilde means "random"

\(\tilde{a}\): a random variable.  \(a\): a value it can take.  (Carlos writes random variables with Greek letters: \(\xi, \eta\).)

pmf and pdf

\(p_{\tilde{a}}(a) = P(\tilde{a} = a)\): the pmf of a discrete \(\tilde{a}\).  \(f_{\tilde{a}}\): the pdf of a continuous \(\tilde{a}\).

Vectors

\(\tilde{x} = (\tilde{x}[1], \dots, \tilde{x}[d])\): a random vector.  \(x[j]\): entry \(j\) of the vector \(x\).

A probability space is \((\Omega, \mathcal{C}, P)\): \(\mathcal{C}\) is the collection of events (Carlos writes \(\mathcal{F}\)).

Recall: conditional probability

A probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{C}, P)\): outcomes \(\Omega\), events \(\mathcal{C}\), probability \(P\).

Conditional probability

\(A, B \in \mathcal{C}\), \(\;P(A) \neq 0\): \(\qquad P(B \mid A) := \dfrac{P(B \cap A)}{P(A)}\)

Chain rule (two events)

\(P(A \cap B) = P(A)\, P(B \mid A)\)

Partition

\(A_1, \dots, A_c\): disjoint, and \(A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_c = \Omega\). Exactly one of them happens.

Recall: Bayes' rule

Law of total probability

If \(A_1, \dots, A_c\) is a partition of \(\Omega\), then for every event \(B\):

\[ P(B) = \sum_{k=1}^{c} P(A_k)\, P(B \mid A_k) \]

Bayes' rule

If \(P(B) > 0\): \(\;P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A)\, P(B \mid A)}{P(B)}\).  With a partition, for every \(y = 1, \dots, c\):

\[ P(A_y \mid B) = \frac{P(A_y)\, P(B \mid A_y)}{\sum_{k=1}^{c} P(A_k)\, P(B \mid A_k)} \]

Why: \(\;P(A_y \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A_y \cap B)}{P(B)} = \dfrac{P(A_y)\,P(B \mid A_y)}{P(B)}\;\) (conditional probability, then the chain rule), and \(P(B)\) from the law of total probability.

The question, in our notation

\(B\) = "the essay is flagged",   \(A_1\) = "written by AI",   \(A_2\) = "written by a human".

\(A_1, A_2\) is a partition: each essay is written by AI or by a human.

Given:   \(P(A_1) = 0.01, \;\; P(A_2) = 0.99, \;\; P(B \mid A_1) = 0.90, \;\; P(B \mid A_2) = 0.09\)

\(P(B) = 0.01 \cdot 0.90 + 0.99 \cdot 0.09 = 0.009 + 0.0891 = 0.0981 \;>\; 0\)

\(P(A_1 \mid B) = \dfrac{0.009}{0.0981} \approx 0.092\)   →   9.2%

Prior and posterior

\( \underbrace{P(A_y \mid B)}_{\text{posterior}} \;=\; \dfrac{\overbrace{P(A_y)}^{\text{prior}} \;\; \overbrace{P(B \mid A_y)}^{\text{update}}}{\underbrace{P(B)}_{\text{normalization constant}}} \)
priorthe belief in \(A_y\) before we see the evidence
posteriorthe updated belief in \(A_y\) after we see the evidence \(B\)
belief\(A_y\): what we want to know but cannot see
evidence\(B\): what we see

The essays: prior \(P(A_1) = 1\%\)  →  posterior \(P(A_1 \mid B) \approx 9.2\%\).

What if 10% of essays were written by AI?

The same detector: \(P(B \mid A_1) = 0.90, \;\; P(B \mid A_2) = 0.09\).

The new prior: \(P(A_1) = 0.10, \;\; P(A_2) = 0.90\).

\(P(B) = 0.10 \cdot 0.90 + 0.90 \cdot 0.09 = 0.09 + 0.081 = 0.171\)

\(P(A_1 \mid B) = \dfrac{0.09}{0.171} \approx 0.526\)   →   52.6%

Prior 1%  →  posterior 9.2%
Prior 10%  →  posterior 52.6%

  • Classification: assign a class to an example, from its features.
  • Supervised learning: we learn from \(n\) examples \((x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_n, y_n)\): the features \(x_i\) and the true class \(y_i\), the label, given by people.
  • Classifier: the rule we learn from these data.

The goal: a conditional pmf

We model an example with random quantities: its features \(\tilde{x}\), and its class \(\tilde{y}\).

Goal

Estimate, from the data, the conditional pmf of the class given the features:

\[ p_{\tilde{y} \mid \tilde{x}}(y \mid x) = P(\tilde{y} = y \mid \tilde{x} = x) \]

Then classify: the MAP estimator ("maximum a posteriori")

\[ \mathrm{MAP}(x) := \arg\max_{y}\; p_{\tilde{y} \mid \tilde{x}}(y \mid x) \qquad \text{the most likely class} \]

If the conditional pmf is correct, no other rule has a smaller probability of error.

Bayes' rule again: the partition \(A_y = \{\tilde{y} = y\}\), the evidence \(B = \{\tilde{x} = x\}\).

Trap question: a lazy filter

In our SMS data, 87.4% of the messages are ham. A filter answers ham for every message, without reading it. What is its accuracy? Is it a good filter?

Accuracy is misleading when one class is rare. So we look at precision and recall.

Attempt 1: just count

Conditional pmf = joint pmf / marginal pmf: \(\qquad p_{\tilde{y} \mid \tilde{x}}(y \mid x) = \dfrac{p_{\tilde{x}, \tilde{y}}(x, y)}{p_{\tilde{x}}(x)}\)

Estimate both by counting the training messages (the empirical joint pmf):

\[ p_{\tilde{y} \mid \tilde{x}}(y \mid x) \approx \frac{\text{number of training messages with counts } x \text{ and class } y}{\text{number of training messages with counts } x} \]

"free money": seen once, spam  →  \(\frac{1}{1}\): spam for sure.   "free win money meeting": \(\frac{0}{0}\): no answer!

The curse of dimensionality

Only 15 of the 1,035 test messages have the same counts as a training message; the other 1,020 give \(\frac{0}{0}\).  With 7,666 words: \(7{,}666^{10} \approx 7 \cdot 10^{38}\) possible 10-word messages, and only 4,136 training messages.

Recall: conditional independence

Conditionally independent given the class

The discrete random variables \(\tilde{x}[1], \dots, \tilde{x}[d]\) are conditionally independent given \(\tilde{y}\) if, for all \(x\) and \(y\):

\[ p_{\tilde{x} \mid \tilde{y}}(x \mid y) = \prod_{j=1}^{d} p_{\tilde{x}[j] \mid \tilde{y}}(x[j] \mid y) \]

Once the class is known, one feature tells nothing more about another.

Rain in Coos Bay and rain in John Day (Oregon) are dependent. Given the rain in Corvallis, which lies between them, much less so.  Independence does not imply conditional independence, nor the reverse.

Attempt 2: build the joint pmf from small pieces

Chain rule:  \(p_{\tilde{x}, \tilde{y}}(x, y) = p_{\tilde{y}}(y)\, p_{\tilde{x} \mid \tilde{y}}(x \mid y)\)

Naive Bayes assumption: the features are conditionally independent given the class:  \(p_{\tilde{x}, \tilde{y}}(x, y) = p_{\tilde{y}}(y) \displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{d} p_{\tilde{x}[j] \mid \tilde{y}}(x[j] \mid y)\)

Marginal pmf:  \(p_{\tilde{x}}(x) = \sum_{k} p_{\tilde{x}, \tilde{y}}(x, k)\),  the sum over the classes.

Naive Bayes

\[ p_{\tilde{y} \mid \tilde{x}}(y \mid x) = \frac{p_{\tilde{y}}(y) \prod_{j=1}^{d} p_{\tilde{x}[j] \mid \tilde{y}}(x[j] \mid y)}{\sum_{k} p_{\tilde{y}}(k) \prod_{j=1}^{d} p_{\tilde{x}[j] \mid \tilde{y}}(x[j] \mid k)} \]

We only need the prior \(p_{\tilde{y}}\) and one small pmf per feature and class.  For messages, the naive assumption is made on the words, not on the counts: next slide.

Why the multinomial distribution?

Given the class \(y\) and the length \(s\), the \(s\) words are drawn one at a time, independently, each from the same pmf on \(V\).  \(q_{y,j}\): the probability that a word of a class-\(y\) message is the \(j\)th word of \(V\).

Like rolling a die with \(\lvert V \rvert\) faces \(s\) times. The counts \(x[1], \dots, x[\lvert V \rvert]\) have the multinomial distribution:

The multinomial model of a message

Assumptions: (1) given the class and the length, the words are independent, with the same pmf \(q_{y,1}, \dots, q_{y,\lvert V \rvert}\) at every position; (2) the length \(s\) has the same pmf in both classes.

\[ p_{\tilde{x} \mid \tilde{y}}(x \mid y) = P(\text{the length is } s \mid \tilde{y} = y) \cdot \frac{s!}{\prod_{j=1}^{\lvert V \rvert} x[j]!}\, \prod_{j=1}^{\lvert V \rvert} q_{y,j}^{\,x[j]} \]

2 faces: the binomial distribution.  The counts are not conditionally independent given the class: they add up to \(s\).  "Naive": real words are not independent.

The naive Bayes classifier

  1. Estimate the prior \(p_{\tilde{y}}\) from the data.
  2. Estimate \(q_{y,j}\) for every word \(j\) of \(V\) and every class \(y\).
  3. Classify with the MAP estimator.

\(\mathrm{MAP}(x) = \displaystyle\arg\max_{y}\; p_{\tilde{y}}(y) \prod_{j=1}^{\lvert V \rvert} q_{y,j}^{\,x[j]}\)

The denominator \(p_{\tilde{x}}(x)\), \(P(\text{the length is } s \mid \tilde{y} = y)\) (assumption 2) and \(\frac{s!}{\prod_j x[j]!}\) are the same for both classes: they cannot change which class wins.

After training, \(p_{\tilde{y}}\) and \(q_{y,j}\) stand for their estimates from the training set, as in the book.

Training = counting

Prior, by the empirical pmf:  \(p_{\tilde{y}}(y) = \dfrac{n_y}{n}\):   \(p_{\tilde{y}}(\text{ham}) = \dfrac{8}{12}, \;\; p_{\tilde{y}}(\text{spam}) = \dfrac{4}{12}\)

\(\text{count}(j, y) = \sum_{i:\, y_i = y} x_i[j]\): how often the \(j\)th word of \(V\) appears in all training messages of class \(y\).

"free win money meeting": ham or spam?

0 against 0: the model cannot decide!

One word never seen in a class makes the whole product 0, whatever the other words say.

The fix: Laplace smoothing

Add 1 to every count

\[ q_{y,j} = \frac{\text{count}(j, y) + 1}{\sum_{j'=1}^{\lvert V \rvert} \text{count}(j', y) + \lvert V \rvert} \]

ham:   \(\dfrac{8}{12} \cdot \dfrac{2}{20} \cdot \dfrac{1}{20} \cdot \dfrac{3}{20} \cdot \dfrac{7}{20} \approx 0.000175\)    spam:   \(\dfrac{4}{12} \cdot \dfrac{5}{16} \cdot \dfrac{4}{16} \cdot \dfrac{5}{16} \cdot \dfrac{1}{16} \approx 0.000509\)

\(p_{\tilde{y} \mid \tilde{x}}(\text{spam} \mid x) = \dfrac{0.000509}{0.000509 + 0.000175} \approx\) 0.74  → spam.

A word not in \(V\) at all (an unknown word) is simply removed from the message.

One more problem: underflow

A real message has many words. A product of many small numbers becomes exactly 0.0 in the computer.

In the notebook: a message of 600 words → product of probabilities = 0.0,  sum of logarithms = −944.9.

The classifier, in logarithms

\[ \mathrm{MAP}(x) = \arg\max_{y} \Big[ \log p_{\tilde{y}}(y) + \sum_{j=1}^{\lvert V \rvert} x[j] \log q_{y,j} \Big] \]

The logarithm is increasing, so the same class wins.  \(\log\) = natural logarithm.

A first look at the data

Setosa is easy. Versicolor and virginica overlap a little: there the machine will make its mistakes.

Recall: pdf and the Gaussian distribution

Words were counted: 0, 1, 2, …  Lengths are real numbers. We need densities.

pdf

A continuous \(\tilde{a}\) has a pdf \(f_{\tilde{a}} \ge 0\): \(\qquad P(\tilde{a} \in [u, v]) = \displaystyle\int_{u}^{v} f_{\tilde{a}}(a)\, da\)

Probability = area under the pdf.  The whole area is 1.  \(P(\tilde{a} = a) = 0\) for every \(a\).

Gaussian (normal) distribution

\(f_{\tilde{a}}(a) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\, \exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(a - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)\)

mean \(\mu\),  standard deviation \(\sigma\)

Trap question: a probability of 3.82?

Setosa petal width: mean 0.25 cm, standard deviation 0.10 cm. Its Gaussian pdf at 0.25 cm is 3.82. So the probability that a setosa petal is exactly 0.25 cm wide is 3.82?

No. 3.82 is a density, not a probability. A pdf can be larger than 1.

Exactly 0.25 cm: probability 0.  An interval has a probability. In the fitted Gaussian model:  \(P(0.24 \le \tilde{x}[4] \le 0.26 \mid \tilde{y} = \text{setosa}) \approx 0.076 \approx 3.82 \cdot 0.02\)

area ≈ height × width

Attempt 1 again: can we count flowers?

Count the training flowers equal to \(x\), as for the messages?

The measurements are real numbers: \(P(\tilde{x} = x) = 0\). There is no pmf of \(\tilde{x}\) to count.

Even rounded to 0.1 cm, as in the data: the 4 features take 35, 23, 43 and 22 different values, so \(35 \cdot 23 \cdot 43 \cdot 22 = 761{,}530\) possible flowers. We have 150.

The curse of dimensionality, again.  So: attempt 2, with densities.

Bayes' rule with a density

Bayes' rule needs \(P(B) > 0\).  But \(P(\tilde{x} = x) = 0\) for every \(x\)!

Bayes' rule with a density

If, given each class \(y\), \(\tilde{x}\) has a conditional pdf \(f_{\tilde{x} \mid \tilde{y}}(\cdot \mid y)\):

\[ p_{\tilde{y} \mid \tilde{x}}(y \mid x) = \frac{p_{\tilde{y}}(y)\, f_{\tilde{x} \mid \tilde{y}}(x \mid y)}{\sum_{k} p_{\tilde{y}}(k)\, f_{\tilde{x} \mid \tilde{y}}(x \mid k)} \]

\(p_{\tilde{y}}(y)\, f_{\tilde{x} \mid \tilde{y}}(x \mid y)\) plays the role of the joint pmf.  The same story: posterior = prior × update / normalization constant.

Why: replace \(\{\tilde{x} = x\}\) by a tiny box around \(x\). Its probability ≈ density × size of the box, and the size cancels in the fraction.

The naive Bayes assumption, again

A density of 4 variables is hard to estimate from 150 flowers.  So we assume:

Naive Bayes assumption

Given \(\tilde{y} = y\), the 4 measurements are conditionally independent:

\[ f_{\tilde{x} \mid \tilde{y}}(x \mid y) = \prod_{j=1}^{4} f_{\tilde{x}[j] \mid \tilde{y}}(x[j] \mid y) \]

The same product as for the words, now with pdfs.

Spam: the words, given the class.  Iris: the 4 measurements, given the species.  The same idea.

Gaussian naive Bayes

Gaussian: given \(\tilde{y} = y\), each \(\tilde{x}[j]\) is Gaussian with mean \(\mu_{y,j}\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_{y,j}\).  3 × 4 = 12 Gaussians.

\(f_{\tilde{x} \mid \tilde{y}}(x \mid y) = \displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{4} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_{y,j}}\, \exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x[j] - \mu_{y,j})^2}{2\sigma_{y,j}^2}\Big)\)

The denominator is the same for all species: it cannot change the winner.

The classifier, in logarithms

\[ \mathrm{MAP}(x) = \arg\max_{y} \Big[ \log p_{\tilde{y}}(y) + \sum_{j=1}^{4} \log f_{\tilde{x}[j] \mid \tilde{y}}(x[j] \mid y) \Big] \]

The book (section 6.5) uses one Gaussian for the whole vector (Gaussian discriminant analysis); naive Bayes, one per feature.

Recall: the correlation coefficient \(\operatorname{corr}(\tilde{x}[j], \tilde{x}[j']) \in [-1, 1]\).  Independent \(\Rightarrow\) corr = 0.  So corr far from 0 means not independent.

Part 4

The MNIST classification problem

70,000 handwritten digits. How does the demo read them?

The data: MNIST

  • 70,000 images of handwritten digits 0–9: a training set of 60,000 and a test set of 10,000. We keep this split.
  • Written by Census Bureau employees and by high-school students.
  • Each digit was resized to fit in a \(20 \times 20\) box, then placed in a \(28 \times 28\) image with its center of mass in the middle.

An example is now an image.  \(\tilde{y}\): the class, now one of the \(c = 10\) digits \(0, 1, \dots, 9\).

The training set is balanced: each digit is 9.0% to 11.2% of it.

A first look at the data

One image = \(28 \times 28 = 784\) pixels. Each pixel has a gray level from 0 (white) to 255 (black ink).

All 60,000 training images: 80.9% of the pixels are exactly 0, and most dark pixels are near 255. Almost black or white.

Black or white: binarization

Pixels \(j = 1, \dots, 784\), row by row: pixels 1 to 28 are the first row.  \(d = 784\) features.

\(\tilde{x}[j] = 1\) if pixel \(j\) has ink (gray level above 127), \(\tilde{x}[j] = 0\) if not.

\(\tilde{x} = (\tilde{x}[1], \dots, \tilde{x}[784])\): the random vector of the pixels.  \(x\): its observed value, one image.

127, the middle of 0–255, is our choice. The demo uses the same.

Recall: the Bernoulli distribution

Bernoulli distribution

The pmf of a Bernoulli random variable \(\tilde{a}\) with parameter \(\theta \in [0, 1]\):

\[ p_{\tilde{a}}(0) = 1 - \theta, \qquad p_{\tilde{a}}(1) = \theta \]

In one formula

\[ p_{\tilde{a}}(a) = \theta^{a} (1 - \theta)^{1 - a}, \qquad a \in \{0, 1\} \]

\(a = 1\): \(\theta^1 (1 - \theta)^0 = \theta\).  \(a = 0\): \(\theta^0 (1 - \theta)^1 = 1 - \theta\).

Carlos writes \(\mathrm{Ber}(p)\).

Why Bernoulli? Why not Gaussian?

After binarization, a pixel has only 2 values. A distribution on \(\{0, 1\}\) is fixed by one number, the probability of 1: it is Bernoulli with parameter \(\theta\).

Why not a Gaussian for each gray level, as for the iris flowers?

  • Gray levels are not bell-shaped: almost white or almost black.
  • Digit 0: 219 of the 784 pixels have the same gray level in all 5,923 training zeros. Then \(\sigma = 0\), and the Gaussian pdf divides by \(\sigma\)!

Trap question: how many numbers?

To know \(p_{\tilde{x} \mid \tilde{y}}(x \mid y)\) for every possible image \(x\) of a digit \(y\), how many numbers do we need?

One for every possible image: \(2^{784} \approx 10^{236}\).  We have 60,000 training images.

Most images would never be seen even once: the curse of dimensionality.  (The book: 16 votes, \(2^{16} = 65{,}536\) voting records, 425 representatives.)

With the naive Bayes assumption: 784 numbers per digit.  \(10 \times 784 + 10 = 7{,}850\) numbers in total: the demo's footer.

The naive Bayes assumption, again

Naive Bayes assumption

Given \(\tilde{y} = y\), the pixels \(\tilde{x}[1], \dots, \tilde{x}[784]\) are conditionally independent:

\[ p_{\tilde{x} \mid \tilde{y}}(x \mid y) = \prod_{j=1}^{784} p_{\tilde{x}[j] \mid \tilde{y}}(x[j] \mid y) \]

Bernoulli naive Bayes model

And given \(\tilde{y} = y\), each \(\tilde{x}[j]\) is Bernoulli with \(\theta_{y,j} = p_{\tilde{x}[j] \mid \tilde{y}}(1 \mid y)\), the probability of ink:

\[ p_{\tilde{x} \mid \tilde{y}}(x \mid y) = \prod_{j=1}^{784} \theta_{y,j}^{\,x[j]} (1 - \theta_{y,j})^{1 - x[j]} \]

\(10 \times 784 = 7{,}840\) Bernoulli distributions: one for each digit and pixel.

The Bernoulli naive Bayes classifier

Bayes' rule with the partition \(\{\tilde{y} = 0\}, \dots, \{\tilde{y} = 9\}\) and \(B = \{\tilde{x} = x\}\).  Here \(P(B) > 0\): probabilities again, not densities.

The classifier, in logarithms

\[ \mathrm{MAP}(x) = \arg\max_{y} \Big[ \log p_{\tilde{y}}(y) + \sum_{j=1}^{784} \Big( x[j] \log \theta_{y,j} + (1 - x[j]) \log(1 - \theta_{y,j}) \Big) \Big] \]

The expression in the brackets: the log score of the digit \(y\). The largest log score wins.

No ink is evidence too!  A pixel without ink brings \(\log(1 - \theta_{y,j})\).

The spam filter was different: a word that is not in the message has \(x[j] = 0\), and adds \(0 \cdot \log q_{y,j} = 0\).

Training = counting

Prior, by the empirical pmf:  \(p_{\tilde{y}}(y) = \dfrac{n_y}{n}\),  from 0.090 (digit 5) to 0.112 (digit 1).

\(\theta_{y,j}\): the fraction of the training images of \(y\) with ink at pixel \(j\):  \(\dfrac{n_{y,j}}{n_y}\),  \(n_{y,j}\) = how many of them have ink there.

Digit 1: 309 pixels never have ink in the 6,742 training 1s.  A new 1 with ink at one of them gets probability 0.

Without smoothing: 32 test images get probability 0 for their true digit, and 4 get 0 for all ten digits (Bayes' rule gives 0/0).

Laplace smoothing: add 1 to the 2 counts, ink and no ink

\[ \theta_{y,j} = \frac{n_{y,j} + 1}{n_y + 2} \]

Always inside \((0, 1)\).  (Spam: add 1 to each of the \(\lvert V \rvert\) word counts.)

How the demo works

  1. You draw. The page cleans up your strokes the way MNIST was made: a \(20 \times 20\) box, then the center of mass in the middle of \(28 \times 28\).
  2. Binarization: \(x[j] = 1\) if the gray level is above 127. "What the machine sees".
  3. The log score of each digit \(y = 0, \dots, 9\), with the 7,850 numbers from training.
  4. Bayes' rule: \(\displaystyle p_{\tilde{y} \mid \tilde{x}}(y \mid x) = \frac{e^{\text{log score of } y}}{\sum_{k} e^{\text{log score of } k}}\). These are the bars.

"Why": each pixel's term in the log score of the winner, minus its term in the log score of the runner-up.  Before you draw, the bars show the prior \(p_{\tilde{y}}(y)\): a rule of the demo, not of the model.

A generative model: the joint pmf

Today we built the joint pmf, by the chain rule and the naive Bayes assumption:

\[ p_{\tilde{x}, \tilde{y}}(x, y) = p_{\tilde{y}}(y) \prod_{j=1}^{784} p_{\tilde{x}[j] \mid \tilde{y}}(x[j] \mid y) \]

The model knows the joint distribution of the digit and the image. So it can generate images:

  1. choose a digit \(y\)  (for example, with probability \(p_{\tilde{y}}(y)\));
  2. draw each pixel on its own: \(x[j] = 1\) with probability \(\theta_{y,j}\), independently.

A generative model: it models the full joint distribution of the labels and the features. The spam filter and the iris classifier are generative models too.
